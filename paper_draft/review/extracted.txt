A Human–AI Readout Direction Changes Perceived
Writing Style but Fails Preservation Tests
Ari Holtzman and NeuriCo
Abstract
Does a direction that separates human and machine text also control the style of new generations? We test
this question in paired base and instruction Qwen2.5-1.5B checkpoints using source-matched continuations,
signed residual additions, projection ablation, five equal-norm random controls, and nuisance residualization.
We evaluate 1,472 generated task outputs with a frozen encoder detector and a blinded external judge, along-
side coherence, factual coverage, and unsupported claims. At the validation-selected layer, negative steer-
ing reduces judge-rated AI-likeness by 20.04 points on a 100-point scale (95% paired bootstrap interval:
[−22.85, −17.27]). However, coherence falls by 0.591 points on a five-point scale, and neither prespecified
preservation criterion passes. The encoder does not corroborate the primary effect; positive steering increases
judge-rated AI-likeness while decreasing its detector score. Readout is strong in both base and instruction
backbones (held-out AUROC 0.9707 and 0.9704), but the instruction-backbone direction fails to transfer to
base-generator continuations (0.486). Removing five nuisance proxies retains a smaller judged style effect
with continued quality loss. These results establish partial control of perceived writing style under this proto-
col, while leaving a distinct, detector-consistent, content-preserving direction unestablished. They show why
readout, controllability, measurement validity, and preservation require separate tests.
1
Introduction
A model can recognize patterns in machine-written text without using the same patterns to produce
its own writing. Distinguishing these two possibilities matters for interpreting representation-based
detectors: an accurate linear readout does not by itself identify a controllable writing mechanism.
Even when an intervention lowers an AI-style score, it may do so by damaging coherence, removing
facts, or exploiting weaknesses in the evaluator.
Can reading out AI style predict control of new writing? Linear probes and mean-difference
detectors extract substantial human–machine information from frozen language-model activations
[Quaremba et al., 2026, Vishnyakov and Gaintseva, 2026]. Prior sparse-autoencoder work also steers
individual stylistic features, with factual side effects at strong doses [Kuznetsov et al., 2025]. The
unresolved question is therefore more specific than whether style can be steered: can a human–
AI readout direction change independent judgments of new text while preserving coherence and
content, and can this effect be distinguished from nuisance style and persona factors?
We test this conjunction in paired pretrained and instruction-tuned QWEN2.5-1.5B checkpoints.
We fit directions to human and GPT-4o continuations from matched source documents, select a
layer on validation readout accuracy, and intervene during generation on 64 unseen factual records.
Figure 1 summarizes the protocol. Signed doses, projection ablation, equal-norm random directions,
nuisance controls, and a plain human-style prompt separate several explanations for a score change.

Related Work
Representation-based detection. Quaremba et al. use last-token residual features and linear probes
to detect machine-generated text across evaluation settings [Quaremba et al., 2026]. SV-DETECT
estimates layerwise directions and combines their features for classification [Vishnyakov and Gaint-
seva, 2026]. These methods establish useful readout structure, but their detection results do not
establish generation control. We use a single-layer direction in native residual coordinates, enabling
a direct intervention rather than treating a multi-layer detector as one physical vector.
Causal steering and persona. Contrastive Activation Addition averages paired residual differences
and applies signed interventions to generation [Rimsky et al., 2024]. Kuznetsov et al. already steer
sparse-autoencoder decoder features associated with complexity, verbosity, formality, and repetition
[Kuznetsov et al., 2025]. Their factual side effects motivate our quality tests; we do not claim the first
causal AI-style intervention. The Assistant Axis characterizes and intervenes on assistant persona
across many roles [Lu et al., 2026]. We include an assistant-versus-narrator output-text proxy in the
tested model, but this reduced control does not reproduce that multi-role axis. Our contribution is
the combined intervention, measurement, and preservation test, including its negative findings.
Matched corpora and distribution shift. HAP-E compares human and model continuations from
shared context, revealing grammatical and rhetorical differences across generators and domains
[Reinhart et al., 2025]. HC3 matches human and ChatGPT answers by question [Guo et al., 2023].
Such matching controls context or topic, not every factual proposition. RAID and MAGE docu-
ment difficulties under changes in generators, domains, decoding, and attacks [Dugan et al., 2024,
Li et al., 2024]. We accordingly separate instruction- and base-generator transfer. Fast-DetectGPT
provides a likelihood-based alternative to encoder detection [Bao et al., 2024]; we did not run it.
Instead, we evaluate a historical encoder and an external style judge, and explicitly test their validity
under the study’s distribution shift.
3
Methodology
3.1
Question, models, and data
We operationalize “sounds like AI” as a measured property of text, not a claim about authorship.
The primary estimand is the prompt-paired change under negative steering in each of two external
style scores. The stronger hypothesis additionally requires preserved coherence and factual coverage
and evidence against the tested nuisance explanations.
2

We use QWEN2.5-1.5B base and instruction checkpoints with frozen weights [Qwen Team, n.d.a,n].
These historical 1.5B checkpoints permit paired interventions with accessible internals; they are not
contemporary capability leaders. Section A provides revisions and runtime details.
Readout corpus. From HAP-E, we join human chunk 2 and generated continuations by source ID;
human chunk 1 is context, not a comparison response. Academic, blog, and news sources provide
384 training, 96 validation, and 128 test documents. A further 128 fiction, spoken, and TV/movie
sources test domain transfer. Fitting uses GPT-4o versus human text only. Each test source also in-
cludes Llama-3-8B base and instruction continuations, sharing the same human reference. Another
128 HC3 question pairs test task transfer without fitting. Together these sets contain 2,240 texts.
We retain the first 128 whitespace-delimited words and at most 192 model tokens; this approximates
rather than guarantees length matching.
Source IDs and normalized hashes are disjoint across folds. A training-vocabulary character-4-gram
TF–IDF audit finds maximum test-to-training cosine 0.580 and no values above 0.90. These checks
cannot rule out semantic overlap or pretraining contamination.
Generation tasks.
We construct 64 fictitious factual records: eight variants in each of eight
community-event topics. Prompts request a 70–100-word paragraph preserving the location, event,
day/time, coordinator, item count, free attendance, and registration deadline, without extra claims.
The records are procedural test inputs; all model outputs come from actual inference. The shared
templates limit the diversity of the 64 inferential units.

We score generated text with the frozen Hello-SimpleAI/chatgpt-detector-roberta Chat-
GPT probability [Hello-SimpleAI, n.d.] and a Gemini judge (google/gemini-3.8-flash). Style
scoring excludes prompts and intervention labels; quality scoring additionally receives the source

The judge rates AI-likeness (0–100), coherence (1–5), source-fact coverage (0–1), and
unsupported-claim fraction (0–1). Requests shuffle conditions into batches of eight with opaque
integer IDs. The measures share neither fitted activations nor condition labels; we do not assume
independent errors.
Because the encoder model card documents training on all HC3, we calibrate both measures on 64
held-out HAP-E human/GPT-4o pairs, not HC3. Lexicographic source selection does not balance
every domain. We also re-rate 64 generated outputs under different batching. Counts, truncation,
repeated trigrams, script-mixing flags, and rough anchor substring recall supplement the ratings;
anchor recall is not semantic verification.
3.4
Statistical analysis and preservation criteria
The primary contrast is instruction mean −2 versus zero on both external style scores. We first aver-
age the two seeds within each prompt. We then bootstrap 64 prompts 5,000 times for percentile 95%
confidence intervals (CIs), and use 10,000 paired sign-flip draws with Holm correction across the
two style tests. We report paired dz = ¯d/sd. Sign-flip inference requires exchangeability/symme-
try under the paired null. We treat ordinal coherence ratings as numeric rubric averages. All other
contrasts are exploratory and use unadjusted tests.
Preservation requires the lower CI bound to exceed −0.25 coherence points and −0.05 coverage
units. An inconclusive noninferiority test does not prove damage. Unsupported claims remain nec-
essary because coverage does not exclude invented information. Exploratory checks include indi-
vidual seeds, eight-template cluster resampling, length adjustment, and matched-quality pairs. The
latter two condition on post-treatment quantities and cannot identify an unbiased direct or preserved-
quality causal effect.

Results
4.1
The primary intervention changes judged style but fails preservation
Table 1 reports the two-seed primary comparison. Mean −2 reduces judge AI-likeness by 20.039
points (CI [−22.852, −17.266]; Holm p = 0.00020; dz = −1.728). The encoder changes by
+0.061 (CI [−0.072, +0.193]; Holm p = 0.381), providing no corroboration of the primary reduc-
tion.
Coherence falls by 0.591 points, with its entire CI below the −0.25 preservation margin. This is
clear coherence damage. Coverage changes by −0.0279, but its lower bound −0.0573 also fails
the −0.05 gate; this interval leaves preservation inconclusive rather than establishing a loss beyond
the margin. Unsupported claims change by +0.0141, with an interval spanning zero. Thus, neither
primary quality gate passes.
Signed doses expose measurement disagreement. Positive mean +2 raises judge AI-likeness by
16.93 points (CI [15.10, 18.72]) while lowering encoder probability by 0.459 (CI [−0.545, −0.373]).
Coherence falls by 0.414 and coverage by 0.131. Figure 2 shows the seed-42 curve; intermediate
instruction doses show that score and quality changes depend on magnitude. The two style measures
are not interchangeable operationalizations of AI-like writing.

4.3
Readout transfers unevenly and is not amplified by instruction tuning
Table 3 separates the backbone used to read text from the generator that wrote it. Held-out GPT-4o
AUROC is 0.9707 for the base backbone and 0.9704 for the instruction backbone. Their paired
instruction-minus-base difference is −0.0003 (CI [−0.0051, +0.0053]). We find no amplification in
this metric; this is not an equivalence test for every aspect of representation strength.
For the instruction backbone, in-domain GPT-4o AUROC has CI [0.950, 0.987]. Transfer to Llama-
3 instruction outputs remains strong (0.913), but Llama-3 base outputs are near chance (0.486; CI
[0.423, 0.550]). Held-out domains and HC3 retain substantial readout performance. These are
readout-transfer results, not demonstrations of causal transfer to those tasks.
Base-model negative steering lowers both judge AI-likeness by 12.81 (CI [−18.28, −7.03]) and en-
coder score by 0.244 (CI [−0.365, −0.124]). Its coherence change is −0.094 (CI [−0.320, +0.133]),
which still fails the noninferiority gate. We did not run the extended controls on the base model, and
prompt formats differ across checkpoints.
Partial length and fluency checks. Restricting within-pair token-length differences to at most ten
retains instruction AUROC 0.973 on 64 in-domain pairs and 0.976 on 77 held-out-domain pairs.
Training projections correlate −0.075 with token length and −0.313 with model negative log like-
lihood (NLL). Negative NLL achieves 0.684 AUROC for in-domain GPT-4o, compared with 0.970

4.4
Measurement validity and robustness qualify the causal finding
On 64 held-out HAP-E human/GPT-4o pairs, encoder AUROC is 0.656 (CI [0.585, 0.729]), with
human/AI mean scores 0.033/0.155.
Judge AUROC is 0.965 (CI [0.930, 0.992]), with means
17.42/58.05. The encoder transfers weakly under this domain/generator shift. Its disagreement
limits corroboration rather than decisively refuting every style effect. Across generated outputs,
encoder/judge Spearman correlation is −0.375.
On 64 re-rated outputs, judge AI-likeness rank agreement is 0.661 and mean absolute difference is
10.78 points; coherence agreement is 0.850 with mean absolute difference 0.211. No human-rating
validation was performed. Calibration discriminating known provenance does not validate every
treatment-induced style distinction.
The negative judge effect appears at both seeds (−21.56 and −18.52), with coherence loss at
both. Resampling eight topic/template clusters yields judge CI [−22.70, −17.30] and coherence
CI [−0.793, −0.405]; eight clusters provide limited population coverage. Exploratory length adjust-
ment leaves a judge coefficient near −20.45, which is not an identified direct effect.
Matched-quality filtering retains only 11/64 instruction pairs at seed 42. Their judge change is
−15.45 (CI [−28.64, −1.82]), while the encoder change is +0.301 (CI [−0.032, +0.613]). Only
22 base pairs pass the same filter, with both scores lower. These selected subsets cannot establish
preservation for all prompts; small-sample bootstrap and discrete sign-flip inference can also differ.
5
Discussion
What does the intervention establish? Signed additions causally alter generated wording and an
external model’s perception of AI-like style in this checkpoint and task. Repeated seeds, zero-hook
equivalence, and equal-norm random controls strengthen this conclusion beyond readout alone. The
evidence does not establish the stronger conjunction of detector consistency, preserved content and
coherence, and distinctness. A reduced style score is insufficient when quality declines or another
evaluator reverses direction.
The base-generator transfer failure is particularly informative. A direction trained on GPT-4o versus
human continuations can capture features associated with instruction-generated text without detect-
ing all machine production. Its availability in the pretrained backbone shows that this readout does
not require instruction tuning of the backbone; it does not establish an explicit internal belief about
authorship. Near-equal base/instruction AUROC supplies no evidence of amplification under the
tested metric.
Distinctness remains unresolved. Residualization preserves a smaller judged effect, but the proxy
subspace is incomplete and partly content-contaminated. In particular, the assistant proxy is not the
published Assistant Axis. Orthogonality to five estimated vectors cannot guarantee independence
from persona, formality, domain, length, or fluency. Better vectors, layers, or lower doses could
offer more selective control, but these data do not establish such an operating point.
Limitations. We study one historical 1.5B model family, 64 templated announcement records,
and no human annotators. Base and instruction generation formats differ. The historical encoder
transfers poorly, while the judge has substantial re-rating noise and measures both style and quality.
Strong additions are large relative to residual norm. The baseline itself often violates the requested
length and no-extra-facts instructions. Source matching and lexical audits cannot exclude semantic
overlap or pretraining contamination, and HAP-E filters refusals and incomplete outputs. Common
templates constrain the exchangeability assumption; most comparisons are exploratory. API aliases
and provider infrastructure remain mutable, and exact local tokens can depend on hardware and
software versions. Section A.4 records experiments omitted from the aspirational handoff.
Broader implications. The principal measurement risk is treating reduced detectability as better
or more human writing. Here, positive steering sharply lowers an encoder score while increasing
judged AI-likeness and damaging coverage. Such a result should not be reported as validated detec-
tor evasion, evidence of human authorship, or a basis for accusations about provenance. Mechanis-
7

tic writing interventions need evaluator validation and source-grounded quality checks, particularly
when deployed systems could optimize a weak score. Our cached outputs preserve failures rather
than selecting fluent successes.
6
Conclusion
A human–AI residual readout direction changes new writing and judged AI-like style in our con-
trolled Qwen2.5-1.5B experiment. Negative steering lowers judge AI-likeness by 20.04 points, but
coherence falls, preservation gates fail, and an external encoder does not corroborate the effect.
Strong readout also fails to transfer to base-generator text. We therefore establish partial style con-
trol, while leaving a distinct, detector-consistent, content-preserving direction unestablished.
A decisive follow-up should retain signed, random, nuisance, and prompt controls while using di-
verse naturally grounded tasks, a stronger contemporary paired model family, human ratings, a
detector validated on current generators, and a genuine multi-role Assistant Axis estimate. Layer
and dose should be selected on separate quality-aware validation data, with preservation tested on a
larger untouched set.

A.2
Rating rubric and prompt specification
The judge system instruction defined AI-likeness as how strongly English prose sounds like generic
machine writing, explicitly excluding proof of authorship. Coherence anchors were 1: unintelligible;
2: major problems; 3: understandable but disjointed; 4: clear with minor awkwardness; and 5:
fluent coherent prose. Coverage measured the fraction of supplied source facts accurately preserved.
Unsupported claims measured the fraction of factual claims absent from or contradicting the source.
For source-free calibration items, coverage and unsupported values were fixed by the rubric to 1
and 0. The rubric requested sensitivity to repetition, abrupt endings, contradictions, and omission;
formality alone did not imply machine authorship, and casual style alone did not merit reward. Texts
were to be treated as data rather than instructions. Exact wording and JSON schema are stored in
results/judge_rubric.json.
The standard generation instruction requested one clear 70–100-word announcement paragraph,
preservation of all supplied facts, no additional factual claims, and only the paragraph as output.
One source record described a library reading workshop, coordinator, time, item count, free atten-
dance, and a deadline two days before the event. Complete records and prompts accompany every
generated output. Separate pottery-course records supplied nuisance prompts; no nuisance output
was counted among the 1,472 task outputs.
A.3
Pairing, inference, and selected subsets
For prompt i, let di be the treatment-minus-zero score difference after averaging available paired
seeds. The primary point estimate is 64−1 P
i di; the percentile bootstrap resamples prompt indices
while preserving their within-prompt pairing. A paired sign flip multiplies each di by an inde-
pendently sampled sign. The Monte Carlo two-sided probability uses an add-one correction over
10,000 draws. Only the two primary external-style tests receive Holm adjustment. The exploratory
condition comparisons retain all available paired outputs, including failures.
The matched-quality analysis uses seed 42 pairs with coherence at least 4 in both conditions, abso-
lute coherence difference at most 0.25, and coverage at least 0.95 in both conditions. The instruction
mean −2 subset has 11 pairs and the base counterpart has 22. These filters use treatment outcomes

and do not estimate population preservation. Likewise, length-adjusted regression may remove a
mediator of the intervention. We report these checks only descriptively.
A.4
Deviations from the aspirational handoff
The frozen feasible protocol reduced the original target of at least 300 prompts, three seeds, and
multiple model families to 64 records, two primary seeds, and paired checkpoints from one family.
It used last-token states at three layers, not an exhaustive layer/position study. No SAE analy-
sis, shuffled-label intervention, norm-matched random residualized control, crossed persona/style
prompt experiment, dedicated fluency intervention, or full formality-manipulation validation was
run. Verbosity contributed to the removed nuisance span but was not a standalone steering condi-
tion. We did not run full RAID/MAGE stress tests, an independent likelihood detector, or causal
generation interventions on natural HAP-E continuations. These omissions are not claimed as com-
pleted experiments. Base controls beyond signed doses were not run. The full inference pipeline was
not rerun twice; repeated smoke batches and a repeated complete numerical analysis were checked.

Observed failures
The instruction baseline already has mean unsupported-claim fraction 0.179, and only 14.8% of
its outputs meet the requested 70–100-word range. Under mean −2, the proportion rated below
coherence 4 rises from 5.5% to 21.9%. Under mean +2, detected Chinese/Cyrillic-script mixing
occurs in 3.9% of outputs, versus 1.6% at zero, and truncation occurs in 2.3%. These are descriptive
flags, not grounds for removing outputs.
In the fixed first-library-task comparison recorded in the report, zero invents an anniversary; mean
−2 invents an end time and duration and calls “13:00” noon; mean +2 adds ornate promotion and
a Chinese ending. The example was fixed by prompt order rather than selected for favorable steer-
ing. Other observed failures include invented target audiences or refreshments, omitted registration
deadlines, exaggerated claims, and awkward quantity descriptions. Full paired text and lowest-rated
outputs are preserved in results/paired_examples.json and results/failure_examples.
